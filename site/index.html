Satellite batteries

Small satellites, about the size of a shoebox, circle the Earth every 90 minutes. Volunteers with radio antennas hear their messages, and this page shows whether their batteries are healthy. In sunlight a battery should charge; in the Earth's shadow it drains, and that is normal.

How it works

1
Listen

Volunteers around the world point small antennas at the sky and record the short radio messages each satellite sends. They share them through SatNOGS.

2
Read the numbers

Each message is a few dozen bytes. This project turns them into the battery's voltage, and its current where the satellite sends it.

3
Check against the Sun

In the Earth's shadow a battery is supposed to drain. So the orbit of each satellite is worked out, and a battery is only judged on what is normal for sunlight or shadow.

4
Warn if something is off

If a battery drains in sunlight, drops below its usual level, or a satellite goes quiet for too long, a warning goes out by Telegram and shows on this page.

What this is not. A learning project, not an operations tool. It refreshes every hour, not live. The rules were checked against a few days of real data and have not yet been shown to catch a real fault. Some voltage units are taken from the satellite's published format or assumed from the size of the numbers.

Code and the full story: satnogs-pipeline, with the orbit model of orbitscreen, whose live tracker shows these satellites too. Beacons from SatNOGS and its volunteer ground stations, orbit data from SatNOGS DB and CelesTrak.
